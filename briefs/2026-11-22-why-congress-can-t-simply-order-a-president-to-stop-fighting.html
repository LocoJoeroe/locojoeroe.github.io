Why Congress Can't Simply Order a President to Stop Fighting

The U.S. Constitution is unambiguous: Article I, Section 8 gives Congress, not the president, the sole power to declare war. The last time Congress actually used that power was in 1941, at the start of World War II. Every armed conflict since — Korea, Vietnam, Iraq, Afghanistan — has been fought without a formal declaration, on the president's executive authority as commander in chief.

In 1973, a Congress exhausted by Vietnam passed the War Powers Resolution over President Nixon's veto. Its core rule: a president may deploy troops into hostilities, but must notify Congress within forty-eight hours and must withdraw those forces within sixty days unless Congress declares war or specifically authorizes the action. The law was designed to make the choice unavoidable. In practice, every president since Nixon has disputed whether it even applies to their actions.

Congress has two real tools. The first — invoking the War Powers Resolution's sixty-day clock — still requires passing a joint resolution the president can veto, meaning Congress needs a two-thirds supermajority to actually compel a withdrawal against a sitting president's will. The second, and historically more effective, tool is the power of the purse: simply refusing to appropriate money for a war. The 1973 Case-Church Amendment, which cut off all funding for combat operations in Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos, is the clearest precedent — it contributed directly to the end of U.S. involvement.

So Congress has the tools on paper. What it has rarely mustered is the political will to use them. A concurrent resolution invoking war powers is symbolically powerful but legally fragile. A funding cut is legally bulletproof — but it forces every member to cast a vote that can be framed as 'abandoning the troops.' That political cost is why the sixty-day clock has run, unacknowledged, in multiple conflicts — and why the purse strings, though real, almost never get pulled.
