Why the President Can't Actually Rewrite Immigration Law Alone

The U.S. Constitution gives Congress, not the president, what courts call 'plenary power' over immigration — the nearly exclusive authority to write the rules on who may enter, stay, or be removed. The Supreme Court has affirmed this repeatedly, and lower courts recently struck down parts of the Trump administration's 2025 asylum restrictions on exactly that basis.

Within that framework, Congress has delegated one significant unilateral tool to the White House: Section 212(f) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, which allows the president to suspend the entry of any class of aliens by proclamation, without a vote, if the president finds their entry would be detrimental to U.S. interests. Every administration since Reagan has used it.

But 212(f) has a hard ceiling. Courts have ruled it cannot override other parts of the same statute — including the section that gives people the right to apply for asylum once they reach U.S. soil. To change that right, or to create new removal procedures, only Congress can act.

The president also controls enforcement priorities — who ICE targets, how many agents deploy where, which cases get dropped — without any congressional vote. That's where the real day-to-day leverage sits. But any lasting deal that changes the written rules of asylum or deportation requires an actual law passed by both chambers.
