Why You Can't Just Move a U.S. Military Base Out of Missile Range

The United States operates roughly 750 military bases across more than 80 countries, according to the Pentagon's own Base Structure Report. Their core job is straightforward: put American firepower close enough to a potential crisis that a response takes hours, not weeks. The Congressional Research Service lists the official functions as facilitating rapid military response, deterring adversaries, and reassuring allies — all three require physical presence, which means buildings, runways, fuel depots, and thousands of personnel that cannot be conjured up overnight somewhere else.

Here's the catch that rarely makes the headlines: none of those bases sit on American soil. Every one of them operates under a negotiated agreement with the host country — a Status of Forces Agreement, or SOFA — that spells out exactly what the U.S. can do, what it can't, and crucially, that the host nation can revoke. If a host country decides the base is making it a target, it can ask the U.S. to leave, and the U.S. has to go. That's not a hypothetical; it's happened before, most famously when the Philippines shut down Clark Air Base and Subic Bay in 1992.

Iran's missile arsenal turns that vulnerability into a live problem. A 2020 U.S. government estimate, cited by the Congressional Research Service, puts the range of Iran's largest ballistic missiles at approximately 2,000 kilometers — enough to cover every major U.S. base in the Gulf: Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, the UAE. The missiles exist. The coordinates of the bases are public. The question is no longer whether Iran can hit those bases; it's whether the host governments will keep accepting that risk on America's behalf.

Moving a base sounds obvious until you price it out. The U.S. spends roughly 55 billion dollars a year just maintaining its overseas network. A major air base requires years of construction, diplomatic negotiation, and infrastructure investment that can't be replicated quickly or cheaply somewhere just out of missile range — because there may not be a willing host nation there at all. The strategic logic that made forward bases indispensable in the Cold War assumed the bases themselves were safe. That assumption is now under serious pressure.
