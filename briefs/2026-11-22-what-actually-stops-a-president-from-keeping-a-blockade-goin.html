What Actually Stops a President From Keeping a Blockade Going?

A naval blockade is not a sanction or an embargo — international law classifies it as a belligerent act of war, legally equivalent to an airstrike. That distinction matters enormously for how it ends.

The 1973 War Powers Resolution was designed to prevent exactly this kind of open-ended military action. It requires the president to terminate hostilities within sixty days unless Congress votes to authorize them. That is the law on the books.

In practice, however, no American court has ever enforced the sixty-day deadline. Congress can pass a concurrent resolution directing the president to withdraw forces, but the president can veto any such measure, and a two-thirds override is rarely achievable. Legal scholars note that no domestic enforcement mechanism exists that can compel a president to stand down.

That means a naval blockade runs exactly as long as the president chooses to run it. The exit is real — the commander in chief can lift the order with a single directive — but the door only opens from the inside.
