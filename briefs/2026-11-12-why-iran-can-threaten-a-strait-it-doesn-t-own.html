Why Iran Can Threaten a Strait It Doesn't Own

The Strait of Hormuz is only twenty-one miles wide at its narrowest point, and the navigable shipping channels inside it are just two miles across each way. The International Energy Agency puts daily traffic through the strait at around twenty million barrels of oil — roughly one-fifth of all global petroleum consumption — with almost no alternative bypass route.

Iran holds the entire northern shore. That means every tanker threading one of those two-mile lanes sits squarely within range of Iran's coastal missile batteries, its fast-attack boat swarms, and an arsenal the Congressional Research Service estimates at roughly five-to-six thousand naval mines. You do not need to own international waters to hold a firing position across them.

International law — specifically Articles 37 through 44 of the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea — declares the right of transit passage through straits 'shall not be impeded' and cannot be suspended. The complication: Iran signed but never ratified UNCLOS, and officially disputes that the transit-passage rules are binding on non-parties, calling them a treaty bargain rather than universal custom.

Iran's threat, then, rests on physics more than paperwork. Enforcing the legal right of passage would require the U.S. Navy to sweep mines under live missile fire — a calculation that makes Iran's leverage real even when the law is unambiguous. The legal answer is clear. The military answer is far more complicated.
