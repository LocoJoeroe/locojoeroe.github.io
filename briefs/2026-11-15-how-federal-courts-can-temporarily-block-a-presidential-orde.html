How Federal Courts Can Temporarily Block a Presidential Order

The answer goes back to 1803. When Chief Justice John Marshall decided Marbury v. Madison, he established the principle of judicial review — the power of courts to declare government actions unconstitutional. The National Archives describes it as a cornerstone of the system designed 'to prevent any one branch of the Federal Government from becoming too powerful.'

Article III of the Constitution gives federal courts jurisdiction over all cases 'arising under' federal law. That means any federal district judge — not just the Supreme Court — can hear a challenge to executive action and issue relief. The Colorado Law Review puts it plainly: a district judge has 'authority and subject matter jurisdiction to grant plaintiffs an injunction that bars the federal executive.'

The specific tool is a Temporary Restraining Order, governed by Rule 65 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. According to Cornell Law School's Legal Information Institute, to obtain one, a party must convince the judge they will suffer 'immediate irreparable injury' unless the order is issued. If the judge agrees, that order can freeze the contested action for up to fourteen days — sometimes without even notifying the other side first — while a full hearing is scheduled.

So the judge isn't overruling the president permanently or unilaterally. The TRO is a constitutional pause button: it preserves the status quo while the courts decide if the underlying action is legal. The president can appeal immediately. What it proves is that the check-and-balance architecture built in 1787 — and sharpened in 1803 — still works exactly as designed, even when it's inconvenient for whoever holds the executive pen.
